A strata committee needs a short, regular report from its cleaning contractor showing what was done against the scope, what problems were found and fixed, and anything the committee must decide. It should be readable in a few minutes by a volunteer who does not visit the building every week.

## Why strata reporting is different

Committee members are owners and volunteers. They are accountable to other owners for how levies are spent, but they are not on site daily. Owners notice problems, such as a dirty lift or an overflowing bin room, and raise them at meetings. A report lets the committee answer with facts and see problems before owners do.

## What should the report contain?

- **Results against the scope.** Area by area, were tasks done to standard?
- **Inspection findings.** What the supervisor found, including failures.
- **Issues and fixes.** Complaints from residents or owners, what was done and how quickly.
- **Photos.** Dated photos of hotspots such as the lobby, lifts and bin rooms.
- **Maintenance flags.** Damage, hazards or repair needs the contractor noticed in common areas.
- **Upcoming work.** Periodic tasks planned, such as glass or carpet cleaning.
- **Decisions needed.** Anything that needs committee approval, such as a scope change or a one-off clean.

## How often should it arrive?

Monthly suits most schemes, timed so it can go into the committee meeting pack. Urgent issues should not wait for the report, so agree how the contractor flags them to the committee or the body corporate manager straight away.

## Who should it go to?

Agree one point of contact, often the body corporate manager or the committee chair, so reports and complaints do not scatter. Make sure the report is filed with the body corporate records.

## What should the contract say?

State that reports are provided, how often and in what form, and that the committee can see underlying records.
