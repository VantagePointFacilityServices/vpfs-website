A clean workplace is not a luxury line in the budget. It affects how staff feel about coming to work, how customers and visitors judge your business in the first minute, and how well shared spaces hold up under daily use. It is also easy to overstate. Cleaning is one part of a healthy workplace, alongside ventilation, good hygiene habits and staying home when unwell, and the honest case for it is strongest when it is made without inflated numbers.

This guide is for managers who need to justify cleaning spend to an owner, a board or a committee, or who want to be sure the money buys something real.

## What does cleaning actually do for the business?

It does three things. It reduces the surfaces and shared spaces that help illness spread, in particular washrooms, kitchens and the things many people touch. It signals care to anyone who walks in, whether they are a client, a candidate or a delivery driver. And it protects your assets: floors, carpets and fixtures last longer when they are maintained.

## How do you know it is working?

Ask for a scope that names the high-touch surfaces and washroom tasks, a regular report against it and photos you can check. Then watch for the signals that matter to you: fewer complaints, fewer repeat issues and a space people are comfortable in.
