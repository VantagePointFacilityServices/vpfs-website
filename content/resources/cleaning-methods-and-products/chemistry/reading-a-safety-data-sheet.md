A safety data sheet (SDS) is the manufacturer's document describing a chemical's hazards and how to use, store and handle it safely. As a buyer you do not need to read every line. Check that it is current, that it matches the product used on your site and that the hazards, storage and first-aid sections make sense for how the product is used.

This is written for the person buying or managing cleaning, so you can understand and check what a contractor is doing. It is not a training manual for cleaners.

## What is an SDS and who must have one?

An SDS is supplied by the manufacturer or importer for hazardous chemicals. WorkSafe Queensland explains the [labelling and safety data sheet requirements](https://www.worksafe.qld.gov.au/safety-and-prevention/hazards/hazardous-chemicals/managing-hazchem-risks/labelling-and-safety-data-sheets), including that sheets should be current. A contractor using hazardous chemicals on your site should be able to give you the SDS for each.

## Which sections matter most to you?

SDSs follow a standard layout. The sections most useful to a site manager are:

- **Product identification:** the exact product name and supplier. Check it matches what is on site.
- **Hazard identification:** the hazards, signal word and warnings.
- **Composition:** what is in it, in general terms.
- **First-aid measures:** what to do after skin, eye or swallowing exposure.
- **Handling and storage:** safe use, what to keep it away from and how to store it.
- **Exposure controls and protective equipment:** what the user must wear.
- **Accidental release measures:** what to do after a spill.
- **Disposal considerations.**

## What should you check?

- The date: the sheet should be current, not years old.
- The product name: does it match the bottle on site?
- The hazards: does the contractor know them and use the right protection?
- The storage advice: is it followed where products are kept on your site?
- Whether any product is described as a disinfectant, in which case see [cleaner vs sanitiser vs disinfectant](/resources/cleaning-methods-and-products/chemistry/cleaner-vs-sanitiser-vs-disinfectant/).

## What if the contractor cannot provide one?

Treat it as a red flag for the whole chemical management system. Ask again in writing and do not accept the product on site without it. See [safety data sheets and chemical management](/resources/compliance-and-risk/what-a-compliant-contractor-holds/safety-data-sheets-and-chemical-management/).

## What should you do with the sheets?

Keep them in a folder with your contractor documents and update them when products change. Make sure cleaners know where the sheets are and that your induction covers chemicals. See [contractor induction and site access](/resources/compliance-and-risk/your-duties-as-the-engaging-business/contractor-induction-and-site-access/).
