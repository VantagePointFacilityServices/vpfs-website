Neutral cleaners suit everyday cleaning of most surfaces, alkaline cleaners cut grease and oils, and acid cleaners remove mineral scale and rust. Using the right family for the job protects surfaces and gives a better result. Never mix cleaning products, because some combinations release hazardous gases.

This is written for the person buying or managing cleaning, so you can understand and check what a contractor is doing. It is not a training manual for cleaners.

## What do the terms mean?

Cleaning products are described by where they sit on the pH scale, which measures how acidic or alkaline something is. Neutral products sit near the middle, alkaline products are above and acid products are below. The pH tells you what a product is good at and what it may damage.

## What is each family used for?

| Family | Good at | Typical commercial uses | Take care with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neutral | Everyday soil, gentle on surfaces | Routine floor and surface cleaning, many finished floors | Heavy grease or scale may need something stronger |
| Alkaline | Grease, oils, protein soils | Kitchens, workshops, heavily soiled floors | Can damage some surfaces and finishes, skin and eye hazard |
| Acid | Mineral deposits, scale, rust, soap scum | Washroom descaling, some stone or tile cleaning in the right circumstances | Can damage some stone, grout and metals, hazardous with other chemicals |

## Why does the right match matter?

Using an acid on the wrong stone can etch it. An alkaline cleaner on a delicate finish can strip it. A neutral cleaner on baked-on grease just takes longer. The safety data sheet and the surface manufacturer's advice tell you what is suitable. See [how to read a safety data sheet](/resources/cleaning-methods-and-products/chemistry/reading-a-safety-data-sheet/).

## Why must products never be mixed?

Some combinations, such as certain acids with bleach, can release toxic gas. Staff should never mix products, and should never top up a bottle with a different product. Labels and training are the controls, and your contractor should be able to explain theirs. See [safety data sheets and chemical management](/resources/compliance-and-risk/what-a-compliant-contractor-holds/safety-data-sheets-and-chemical-management/).

## What about dilution?

More is not better. Using too much product wastes money, leaves residue that attracts dirt and increases the hazard. Dosing systems and measured dilution help keep it right.

## What should you ask a contractor?

Which products do they use for which jobs, how are they diluted, how are staff trained and how do they prevent mixing? Ask which family each product belongs to and why it suits your surfaces.
